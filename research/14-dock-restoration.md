## Why this fits him better than most ideas we've looked at

- **No licence threshold in sight.** NC's general-contractor licence bites at **$40,000** per
  project (GS 87-1). Decking replacement on a residential dock is an order of magnitude below that.
  Unlike [[lighting-correction]] (wall dimmers = electrical contracting) and the camera/smart-lock
  idea (alarm licence), there is no obvious trade-licence wall here. ⚠️ *Needs verifying — see below.*
- **No equipment gate.** Circular saw, drill, pry bar, hand tools. This does **not** wait on the
  $10k mini skid steer that gates land-clearing and demo.
- **Two-man shaped.** Ray + Chase, one dock, a day or two. No helpers, no subs.
- **It is the thing he already says he can do.** Milepost already advertises maintenance/repairs
  quietly; "I can fix anything" was his own line.
- ⭐ **Milepost is the lead engine, and it is better here than for lighting.** He is already walking
  waterfront and soundfront properties on paid home-watch visits, with a camera, writing a condition
  report. **Dock decay is visible from thirty feet away** — cupping, greying, popped fasteners, soft
  boards. Every home-watch visit on a waterfront property is a free, already-paid-for dock
  inspection. That is a genuinely strong pairing: zero acquisition cost, and a photo in a report he
  is already sending is the most natural quote lead-in imaginable.
- **OBX has enormous waterfront inventory** and salt + UV destroy decking on a schedule. This is
  recurring work across a stock of houses, not one-off demand.

## The expertise that actually matters (and separates a good job from a callback)

Worth knowing before quoting anything:

- **Fasteners are the whole ballgame in saltwater.** Modern pressure-treated lumber (ACQ / MCA) is
  chemically aggressive toward ordinary galvanised fasteners — it eats them. The correct spec is
  hot-dip galvanised to **ASTM A153** or, near salt, **316 stainless**. Get this wrong and the deck
  looks fine for two years and then fails at every screw. This is the dock-work equivalent of the
  LED dimmer-compatibility problem: unglamorous, technical, and the reason to hire someone.
- Board material choice: PT pine vs composite vs hardwood (ipe), and what each does over water.
- Fastening pattern, gapping for drainage and swell, hidden vs face fastening.
- Cutting over water — dropped tools and offcuts are gone, and offcuts in the sound are a problem.

## ⚠️ Three questions that decide whether this is real

These are the ones that have bitten every previous idea late. Answer them **before** any marketing
copy exists.

1. **CAMA.** Dare County is a Coastal Area Management Act county, and a dock is a structure in
   public trust waters. Repair/maintenance **in kind** is usually treated differently from
   replacement or expansion — there is generally a maintenance exemption — but "generally" is not
   good enough. Need: does like-for-like decking replacement on an existing permitted dock require a
   CAMA permit, an exemption certificate, or nothing at all? Also whether the **Army Corps** or **NC
   Division of Coastal Management** has any say. If the answer is "nothing for in-kind decking, a
   permit the moment you touch a piling," that *validates his own instinct* to stop at the boards.

2. **Does his GL policy cover work over water?** ⚠️ Many general-liability policies carry marine or
   over-water exclusions, and some exclude work on piers/wharves specifically. He has GL and
   believes he is covered for the current lines — that belief should not be extended to standing on
   a dock over the sound with a saw. This is a phone call to his agent, not a research task.

3. **NC sales tax — and I think the earlier finding may NOT carry over.**
   [[obx-websites-conversion-research]] recorded "services NOT sales-taxable" as verified, but that
   was about tree work, clearing and junk hauling. NC taxes **repair, maintenance and installation
   services (RMI)** on real property, with an exception for **real-property contracts** that are
   capital improvements. Replacing dock decking sits right on that line: is it a taxable repair or a
   non-taxable capital improvement? Getting this wrong means either under-collecting (his liability)
   or over-charging customers. **Do not assume the earlier answer applies.**

## Honest risks

- **Falls and drowning.** This is the most physically dangerous line we have considered. Not a
  reason not to do it; a reason to be deliberate about it and to settle question 2 first.
- **Visible failure.** A dock is inspected daily by its owner and by neighbours. Good work advertises
  itself; bad work does too.
- **Structural temptation.** The money and the ask will both drift toward pilings and framing. The
  moment it does, the licence and permit picture changes completely. His "top boards for now" line
  is the guardrail — keep it written down.
- **Scope discovery.** Pull decking and you find rotten stringers. Need a written policy for "we
  opened it up and the frame is gone" *before* the first job, not during it.
