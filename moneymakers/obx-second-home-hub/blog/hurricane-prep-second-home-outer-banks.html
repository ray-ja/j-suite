Hurricane Prep for a Second Home You Can't Get To

Hurricane season on the Outer Banks runs June 1 through November 30 — six months of every year when your coastal home might face a storm you're watching from three states away. If your place sits empty for part of that window, the hardest part isn't the storm itself. It's the helplessness: the radar on your phone, the wondering, the not-knowing.

Here's the reassuring truth. Most storm damage to second homes is preventable, and almost all of it comes down to decisions you make before a storm is ever named. The owners who get through hurricane season calmly aren't lucky — they have a plan, supplies in place, and one local person who can act when they can't. This guide walks you through all three.

Before the season: the prep that actually matters

The single most valuable thing you can do happens in May, not during a warning. Do this once a year:

  • Photograph the entire property, inside and out, and date the photos. This is your insurance baseline. If you ever file a claim, "here's exactly how it looked before" is worth more than any argument.
  • Confirm your insurance is current and understand it. Know your wind and flood coverage and your deductible before you need them. (More on the vacancy side of this in our guide to vacant-home insurance.)
  • Trim trees and identify your projectiles. Anything that can fly in 90 mph wind — furniture, grills, décor, the kayak under the deck — needs a plan and a place to go.
  • Stock your storm supplies now. Flashlights, batteries, tarps, rope, a water-main shutoff tool. Scrambling for these the day before a storm is how things get missed.
  • Decide who secures the house. This is the big one. If a storm forms while you're away — and statistically, it will at some point — who brings in the furniture, closes the shutters, shuts off the water? Decide now, while it's calm.

When a storm is forecast (48–72 hours out)

This is where your prep pays off. With a plan already made, the to-do list is short:

  • Track the system and watch for county evacuation orders. On the Outer Banks, visitor and resident evacuations are ordered separately, and they happen fast. If you have guests or renters, they typically have to leave first.
  • Secure or store everything loose — furniture, grills, umbrellas, beach gear, trash bins, décor.
  • Confirm shutters or window coverings are in place.
  • Shut off the water at the main if the home will be empty. A supply line that bursts during a power outage, with no one there, is one of the most common and most expensive storm claims. Killing the water removes the risk entirely.
  • Check your backups — battery power, sump pump, device charging.
  • Take final photos of the secured home.

After the storm: speed protects you

When it's safe and roads reopen, the priority is getting eyes on the property fast:

  • Damage caught early is cheaper and better for your claim. A roof leak found in hour one is a tarp; found in week three it's a mold remediation.
  • Document everything before you clean up. Photos and video first, always.
  • Check the essentials: roof, windows, water intrusion, flooding, power, HVAC restart.
  • Clear debris before it causes drainage or pest problems.
  • File your claim promptly with your documentation in hand.

The part you can't do from far away

Every step above that says "secure the house" or "get eyes on it fast" assumes someone is physically there. That's the gap. You can do all the planning in the world from your desk, but a storm plan without a local responder is half a plan.

Two things close that gap. Smart monitoring — leak sensors, a temperature monitor, a power-loss alert, a camera — lets you watch your home through the storm from anywhere (as long as you have backup internet that survives the outage). And a local home-watch service is the person who actually secures the property before and inspects it after. Monitoring tells you something's wrong; a local fixes it. Together, they're the real plan.
