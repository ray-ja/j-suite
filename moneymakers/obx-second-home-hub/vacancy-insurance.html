Vacancy & insurance: the empty-home trap

An empty house isn't just a maintenance risk — it can quietly weaken your insurance. Here's what OBX owners need to know, in plain English.

⚖️ Not legal or insurance advice. Policies vary — always read yours and ask your agent. This is a plain-language overview to help you ask the right questions.

Vacancy & unoccupancy clauses

Most homeowner policies treat a home that's been empty for a while differently. After a set period — often 30 to 60 days of no one living there — some policies reduce or exclude coverage for losses like water damage, freezing, vandalism, or theft. The logic: an occupied home gets problems noticed and fixed fast; an empty one doesn't.

For a second home that's empty most of the year, this is the quiet risk. You may be paying premiums while sitting in a coverage gap you didn't know existed. The fix isn't always a different policy — sometimes it's demonstrating the home is actively maintained and checked.

Coastal coverage: wind & flood are separate

  • Flood is never in a standard policy. On the OBX you'll typically carry separate flood coverage (NFIP or private). Lenders usually require it in flood zones.
  • Wind/hurricane may be carved out into its own policy or a separate, higher hurricane deductible.
  • Know your deductibles — coastal wind deductibles are often a percentage of the insured value, not a flat dollar amount, which can be a large surprise.

The periodic-check expectation

Increasingly, carriers expect — and sometimes require — that vacant or seasonal homes get periodic checks. Even when not strictly required, being able to show dated visit logs and photo reports does two valuable things: it supports that you maintained the home (countering a vacancy denial), and it gives you fast, time-stamped documentation when something does go wrong.

💡 This is the underrated reason owners hire home watch. A monthly photo report isn't just peace of mind — it's a paper trail that protects your claim.

What to actually do

  • Call your agent and ask specifically about vacancy/unoccupancy terms for a seasonal home, and whether periodic checks are expected.
  • Confirm flood + wind coverage and your real deductibles.
  • Set up documented checks — even monthly — with photo reports you keep on file.
  • Reduce the loss itself: shut off water when away, add leak sensors, keep HVAC managing humidity. Fewer losses, fewer claims, lower risk of a denial fight.

FAQ

How long can my OBX home sit empty before coverage changes?
It depends on your policy, but 30–60 days is a common threshold for vacancy/unoccupancy provisions. Read your policy and ask your agent about your specific situation.
Will adding leak sensors or monitoring lower my premium?
Some carriers offer discounts for water-shutoff and monitoring devices. Even when they don't, these devices reduce the losses that drive claims and denials — worth it either way.
Can a home-watch report be used in a claim?
Dated visit logs and photos are strong supporting documentation for a claim and for demonstrating the home was maintained. Keep them organized with your policy.
