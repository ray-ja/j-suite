## H1: The Vacancy Clause That Could Sink Your Second-Home Insurance Claim

Most second-home owners assume their insurance works the same whether they're in the house or not. For a lot of policies, that assumption is wrong — and the place you find out is at the worst possible moment, when you file a claim and it gets reduced or denied.

Here's what every Outer Banks owner with a part-time home should understand about how vacancy affects coverage, and what to do about it.

> **A note up front:** this is general information, not insurance or legal advice. Every carrier and policy is different — the specifics of *your* coverage are a conversation with *your* agent. What follows is what to ask them about.

### Why empty homes are treated differently

Insurance is priced on risk, and an unwatched home is riskier than an occupied one. A small leak in a lived-in house gets noticed and fixed in a day. The same leak in an empty house can run for weeks, turning a minor repair into a catastrophic claim. Carriers know this, so many policies contain language that changes — or limits — coverage when a home is left unoccupied beyond a certain period.

### The concepts to know

You don't need to be an insurance expert, but a few terms help you ask the right questions:

- **Vacancy clause.** Many policies reduce or exclude coverage for certain types of damage once a home has been unoccupied for a set length of time (often counted in consecutive days/weeks).
- **Occupancy definitions.** Policies define "occupied" in specific ways — and a vacation home or a between-guests rental may not meet that definition the way you'd assume.
- **"Checked regularly."** Some policies expect a vacant property to be looked in on periodically, and may treat damage differently if it wasn't.

The point isn't to alarm you — it's that these details are usually buried in the policy and rarely come up until a claim does.

### The questions to ask your agent

Take these straight to your insurer. The answers tell you exactly where you stand:

1. How long can my home be unoccupied before my coverage changes?
2. Does my policy have a vacancy clause, and what does it exclude?
3. Do you require or expect periodic checks on the property while it's vacant?
4. If so, what counts as acceptable documentation that checks happened?
5. Are there steps I can take that would maintain — or even improve — my coverage?

### How documentation protects you

Here's the practical takeaway, whatever your specific policy says: **a dated, photo-documented record that someone responsible checked the home and found it sound is one of the strongest things you can have at claim time.** It demonstrates the home wasn't neglected, supports the timeline of when damage occurred, and answers the exact questions an adjuster will ask.

That documentation is, not coincidentally, exactly what a regular home-watch visit produces — a photo report on every visit.
